7     Model Formulation

7.1   Coupled Linear Equations Framework for Optimization (CLEFO)
The biochemical processes involved in biological wastewater treatment are inherently
complex, characterized by multiple interdependent constituents, including various
compounds and biomass concentrations. Here, we propose a coupled linear equations
framework for optimization tailored to encapsulate the intricate interdependencies and
interaction effects inherent in biological wastewater treatment systems. The primary
objective of this model is to provide a tractable yet comprehensive framework that
captures the dynamic relationships among active wastewater constituents, specifically
compound and biomass concentrations, while accounting for the influence of key
operational parameters such as flow rate, media porosity, and influent compound
concentrations without loss of tractability in a global optimization routine.

porosity, and process variables, denoted as 𝐗 𝑝 , and various influent compound
concentrations, denoted as 𝐗 𝑐 . To concatenate 𝐗 𝑝 (process variables) and 𝐗 𝑐 (influent

interdependencies among these dependent variables—where the concentration of one
compound or biomass species may influence others—the model incorporates a coupling
coefficients matrix (𝚪) to represent these mutual interactions. For example, in a biological
wastewater treatment system, the concentration of dissolved oxygen (𝑌1 ) plays a critical
role in the activity and growth of aerobic biomass species, such as nitrifying bacteria (𝑌2 ).
An increase in 𝑌1 can enhance the metabolic activity of nitrifying bacteria, leading to higher
rates of ammonium (𝑌3 ) oxidation to nitrite and nitrate. Conversely, as the nitrification
process progresses and oxygen is consumed, 𝑌1 may decrease, potentially limiting the
activity of the aerobic biomass. This dynamic interplay between dissolved oxygen and
biomass illustrates how changes in one dependent variable can cascade through the
system, influencing others. The coupling coefficients matrix (𝚪) captures these
interdependencies quantitatively, enabling the model to account for such feedback loops
and their effects on the overall biochemical processes.

On the other hand, to capture the interaction effects among independent variables, the
model introduces pairwise interaction terms (𝐙). These are constructed by taking the
product of every unique pair of independent variables, such as the interaction between
flow rate and media porosity (𝑍1 = 𝑋1 × 𝑋2 ), thereby encapsulating synergistic or
antagonistic effects that individual variables alone may not reveal. For instance, in a
biological wastewater treatment system, the interaction between flow rate (𝑋1 ) and media
porosity (𝑋2 ) can significantly influence the efficiency of the treatment process. A high
flow rate might lead to insufficient contact time between the influent wastewater and the
treatment media, potentially reducing the removal efficiency of pollutants. However, if the
media porosity is also high, it could compensate for the reduced contact time by allowing
greater penetration and distribution of wastewater throughout the treatment bed.
Conversely, a combination of low flow rate and low porosity might create stagnant zones,
leading to uneven treatment and localized inefficiencies. By including the interaction term
(𝑍1 = 𝑋1 × 𝑋2 ), the model captures these synergistic or antagonistic effects, reflecting
how the interplay between flow rate and media porosity jointly impacts the treatment
performance in ways that considering these variables individually might miss.
Additionally, the model accounts for interaction effects between independent and
dependent variables through another set of interaction terms (𝐖), defined as the product
of each independent variable with each dependent variable (e.g., 𝑊1 = 𝑋1 × 𝑌1 ). This
allows the influence of an independent variable on a dependent variable to be modulated
by the current state of that dependent variable, effectively capturing feedback
mechanisms within the system. For example, consider the interaction between flow rate
(𝑋1 ) and the concentration of dissolved oxygen (𝑌1 ) in a biological wastewater treatment
system. While a higher flow rate can increase the mixing of oxygen in the system, thereby
potentially raising dissolved oxygen levels, the actual impact depends on the existing
concentration of 𝑌1 . If 𝑌1 is already at a sufficient level, the additional mixing from
increased 𝑋1 may have a negligible effect. Conversely, if 𝑌1 is low, the same increase in
𝑋1 could have a significant positive impact by enhancing oxygen transfer. By including
the interaction term 𝑊1 = 𝑋1 × 𝑌1 , the model captures this feedback mechanism, where
the effect of flow rate on dissolved oxygen is modulated by the current state of 𝑌1 .

7.5      Multi-Stage, Multi-Unit Network Optimization Framework
The single-unit CLEFO optimization model for wastewater treatment is extended to
accommodate a multi-stage, multi-unit framework that captures the complexities of
sequential treatment processes, which is required to solve the problem detailed in
Equation 3-2. In this extended model, the wastewater undergoes 𝑆 sequential treatment
stages 𝑠 = 1, 2, … , 𝑆, with each stage 𝑠 capable of employing one among 𝑈𝑠 alternative
process units 𝑢 ∈ 𝒰𝑠 . This selection is governed by binary decision variables 𝑦𝑠,𝑢 ,
ensuring that exactly one unit is chosen per stage, thereby constructing a superstructure
of feasible unit configurations across all stages.
The model incorporates three distinct types of independent variables 𝑋 namely, the
influent compound concentrations (𝑋 𝐼 ), flow variables (𝑋 𝐹 ), and process-unit-specific
                                                                                   𝐼
variables (𝑋 𝑃 ). For the first stage (𝑠 = 1), influent compound concentrations 𝑋𝑠,𝑖  represent
the raw wastewater concentrations of compound 𝑖 ∈ ℐ. For subsequent stages (𝑠 > 1),
the influent concentrations are directly linked to the effluent concentrations from the
                             𝐼
preceding stage, i.e., 𝑋𝑠,𝑖     = 𝑌𝑠−1,𝑖 . On the other hand, the value of volumetric flow
variables 𝑋𝑓𝐹 are decided exclusively at the first stage for 𝑓 ∈ ℱ are held constant across
all stages to maintain consistent flow rates throughout the treatment process. Lastly,

7.6   McCormick Envelopes
It is apparent that the following linearization employed on bilinear terms of the multi-stage,
multi-unit framework makes the model large due to the multiple linearization steps of the
method and its introduction of binary variables and constraints at each step, which begins
by expressing the bilinear term xy as a difference of two squared auxiliary 𝑤 variables,
i.e., (𝑤1 )2 − (𝑤2 )2 , and then the squared terms are linearized using piecewise linear
approximation through the auxiliary 𝑇 variables, i.e., (𝑤1 )2 ↦ 𝑇1 , (𝑢2 )2 ↦ 𝑇2. For large
applications, the linearization may render the model more computationally costly that its
original nonlinear model. We address this issue by employing a linear relaxation using
McCormick envelopes (McCormick, 1976). We know that 𝑥 and 𝑦 are continuous
variables with known bounds
