2    Theoretical Background

Central to biological wastewater treatment is the fact that continuous supply of matter and
energy is essential to support life through the creation and upkeep of cells. The key
elements involved in this life system include hydrogen (H), oxygen (O), carbon (C) and
nitrogen (N). Based on this, microorganisms involved in biological wastewater treatment
can be categorized based on the three main energy sources they derive from.
Photosynthetic autotrophs rely on radiation as their primary energy source. Heterotrophs
obtain energy from organic compounds. Chemical autotrophs use inorganic compounds
as their energy source.

for the biosphere, and photosynthetic autotrophs capture this energy through
photosynthesis, converting CO₂ and water into glucose and releasing O₂, which supports
other life forms. These autotrophs also require nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus for
growth and metabolism, with nitrogen being essential for proteins and nucleic acids, and
phosphorous for ATP production. However, nutrient availability, especially in aquatic
ecosystems, can limit their growth. Heterotrophs, such as protozoa, fungi, and bacteria,
rely on consuming other organisms or organic matter for energy, which is used to
synthesize their cells, though some energy is lost as heat in the process. This results in
a decrease in available organic energy as it transfers between organisms, eventually
limiting heterotrophic life. Chemical autotrophs, which derive energy from the oxidation of

autotrophs. In wastewater treatment, autotrophs typically refer to these chemical
autotrophs.
2.1   Organics Degradation
Heterotrophic energy metabolism entails the degradation of complex organic compounds
(CxHyOz), releasing CO2, protons (H+), and energy. CO2 is liberated as a gaseous
byproduct, while H+ is sequestered through association with a suitable acceptor in
reduction-oxidation (redox) reactions. Aerobic wastewater treatment employs
microorganisms that utilize O2 as the terminal electron acceptor during the oxidation of
organic compounds, a process known as aerobic respiration. The reaction equation
representing this process are as follows (Xie, 2021):

2.2   Nitrification = Nitritation + Nitratation

derive energy through the oxidation of inorganic N compounds, such as NH4+ and NO2⁻,
during nitrification. Unlike heterotrophs, these autotrophs do not depend on organics for
energy. Consequently, their O2 demand is distinct from the O2 demand associated with
the degradation of organics by heterotrophs. Although O 2 demand experiments are not
required to measure O2 demand during nitrification, given the well-established
stoichiometry of the nitrification process. The sequential reactions involving the oxidation
of NH4+ to NO2⁻ and subsequently to NO3⁻ are detailed. The initial stage of nitrification,
referred to as nitritation, involves the microbial-driven oxidation of NH4+ to NO2⁻. This

2.3   Denitrification
Under anoxic conditions, heterotrophic microorganisms adapt their metabolic processes
due to the absence of O2. Heterotrophic denitrifying bacteria utilize alternative electron
acceptors, such as NO3⁻ or NO2⁻, in a process known as denitrification. This process
plays a pivotal role in N removal, converting NO 3⁻ or NO2⁻ into nitrogen gas (N2), which
is released into the atmosphere. Heterotrophic denitrifying bacteria assimilate NO 3⁻ or
NO2⁻ from their environment and reduce them through a series of sequential steps. The
heterotrophic denitrification process is generally contingent upon the electron donor
involved. The common denitrification reactions occurring in biological wastewater
treatment systems are presented in Table 2-1.

2.5       Biokinetic Modeling
Three things are apparent from the previous discussions. Firstly, multiple interrelated
biochemical reactions are involved in the production and consumption of compounds in
a biological wastewater treatment system. Secondly, these reactions are carried out by
multiple organisms. Lastly, the production and consumption of compounds occur in fixed
proportions. The ASM family of models were developed in the late 1990s in view of these
facts (Barker & Dold, 1997; Dold et al., 1981; Gujer et al., 1999; Henze et al., 1987, 1999,
2006; Hu et al., 2007; Meijer, 2004; Nelson & Sidhu, 2009; Rieger et al., 2001). The
reaction rate terms 𝑅𝑖 (𝐂) in the ASM are interdependent and are governed by the
stoichiometric relationships. The stoichiometric matrix 𝐒 encapsulates the fixed
proportions of consumption and production of compounds during biochemical reactions
(Henze et al., 2006). Specifically, the relationship between the reaction rate vector 𝐑 and
the biokinetic process vector 𝜌(𝐂) is given by:

There are ways to linearize the reaction rate vector 𝐑. The most common approach is the
use Taylor series expansion (M. Li et al., 2020; T. Yang et al., 2014), which is fine for
optimal control applications, but this approach greatly limits the search space for design
optimization applications because the model is only valid around vicinity of the
approximation point. This limitation can be addressed by taking advantage of the structure
of the Monod function. Essentially, 𝜇𝑗 (𝐂) is a product of a constant 𝜇𝑚𝑎𝑥,𝑗 and the

are a multitude of ways to elucidate these correlations, but the important aspect of this
realization is that it allows us to lump the effects of stoichiometric coefficients to the
production and consumption of compounds into correlation coefficients in some empirical
model. To this end, ML models can be used, which is the premise of this study. In fact,
several studies have deployed ML techniques to approximate nonlinearities in biological
wastewater treatment process (Ahn et al., 2014; Asadi et al., 2017; Fang et al., 2011; Kim
et al., 2009; Kusiak & Wei, 2012; Lim et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2016), which substantiates
the theoretical arguments posed here, but these existing approaches are not without
gaps, which are highlighted in the next section.

3     Review Related Literature

3.1    Foundational Activated Sludge Models
The Activated Sludge Model (ASM) family represents one of the most significant
developments in modern wastewater treatment engineering, providing a standardized
framework for simulating and designing biological treatment processes in activated
sludge systems (Henze et al., 2006). Initially developed under the aegis of the
International Association on Water Quality (IAWQ), which eventually became the
International Water Association (IWA), these models consolidated diverse research
efforts and practical experiences into a unified, mathematical representation of critical
microbial and chemical reactions (Henze et al., 2006). The need for such standardized
models arose due to the inherent complexity of biological wastewater treatment: multiple
microbial populations compete and cooperate to remove carbon, nitrogen, and,
eventually, phosphorus from the influent stream (Dold et al., 1981). By creating a common
platform for predicting effluent quality and guiding design modifications, the ASM family
fundamentally transformed both scientific inquiry and operational decision-making in
wastewater engineering (citation needed).(Dold et al., 1981; Henze et al., 1999; Nelson
& Sidhu, 2009; Vanrolleghem et al., 1999)
ASM1 was the first model in this series, introduced in 1987 by Henze, Grady, Gujer,
Marais, and Matsuo (Henze et al., 1987; Van Loosdrecht et al., 2015). It focused primarily
on carbon oxidation and nitrogen removal processes, capturing the essential dynamics
of heterotrophic and autotrophic biomass (Van Loosdrecht et al., 2015). Within ASM1,
heterotrophs metabolize readily biodegradable substrate and oxygen under aerobic
conditions, while they denitrify under anoxic conditions by using nitrate as an electron
acceptor (Henze et al., 2006). Autotrophs, on the other hand, nitrify ammonia to nitrite
and then to nitrate, representing one of the most critical steps in maintaining nitrogen
balance in effluents (Henze et al., 1987, 2006). The ability of ASM1 to predict effluent
quality for key parameters—such as chemical oxygen demand (COD), biochemical
oxygen demand (BOD), ammonia, and nitrate—rendered it a powerful tool for both
researchers and plant operators, even though the model did not explicitly address
phosphorus removal (Henze et al., 1999). Despite its limitations, ASM1’s relatively
straightforward approach to substrate utilization, biomass growth, and decay established
the core framework still evident in later models (Henze et al., 2006; Van Loosdrecht et
al., 2015).
Building on this foundation, ASM2 emerged in the mid-1990s to address enhanced
biological phosphorus removal (EBPR) processes by introducing phosphorus-
accumulating organisms (PAOs) (Henze et al., 1999). This expanded scope reflected the
growing interest among engineers and regulators in controlling eutrophication, a process
driven by excessive nutrient discharge into natural water bodies (Selerio, 2022). Under
