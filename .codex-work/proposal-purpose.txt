4   Statement of the Problem

Over the past two decades, various machine learning (ML) techniques have
demonstrated promise in modeling and optimizing biological wastewater treatment
processes. These approaches can capture nonlinear relationships among operational
parameters, biochemical reactions, and treatment performance indicators. However,
three main challenges persist:
Data Spillage and Oversimplification
Many existing studies include performance indicators (e.g., effluent concentrations) as
direct inputs to ML models, leading to “data spillage.” This undermines model
generalizability and inflates predictive accuracy in ways that do not hold in real operational
settings. Additionally, linear or polynomial models often fail to capture compound
interdependencies, ignoring the coupled biochemical reactions and microbial community
interactions critical to process performance.
Lack of Mechanistic Interpretability
Advanced ML methods (e.g., random forests, neural networks) deliver high predictive
power but function as “black boxes,” making it difficult to extract mechanistic insights. This
lack of transparency limits the ability of process engineers to incorporate domain
knowledge, improve fundamental processes, or troubleshoot effectively when real-world
performance deviates from model predictions.
Scalability and Model Gaps
When extending optimization to large-scale treatment superstructures with multiple units,
sources, mixers, and sinks, the dimensionality of the decision space escalates, causing
heuristic optimizers and complex ML models to struggle with convergence and tractability.
Moreover, no ML method has been validated for modeling advective-diffusive attached-
growth systems (e.g., bio-towers), leaving an important class of wastewater processes
insufficiently studied.
These gaps underscore the need for a modeling framework that:
       Reflects the interconnected nature of multiple wastewater constituents without
        causing data spillage,
       Maintains interpretability by integrating fundamental biochemical relationships,
        and
       Remains computationally tractable for large-scale or superstructure optimization
        tasks.
The research questions that this dissertation will answer are the following:

      How can ML-based models be designed to capture the interdependencies among
       multiple wastewater constituents without incurring data spillage, thereby improving
       predictive accuracy and generalizability?
      What modeling strategies or frameworks can balance the “black-box” nature of
       advanced ML methods with the mechanistic interpretability needed for practical
       process design and troubleshooting?
      How can these improved modeling approaches be scaled up to large, multi-stage

The primary objective of this dissertation is to develop and validate a modeling and
optimization framework that bridges the gap between purely data-driven ML methods and
mechanistic models for biological wastewater treatment. Specifically, the proposed

      Eliminate data spillage by rigorously separating input variables from performance
       metrics in model training and validation, ensuring more trustworthy predictions.
      Enhance mechanistic interpretability by incorporating biochemical and microbial

       with actionable insights.
      Demonstrate scalability through the application of the framework to multi-stage
       systems and superstructures, including advective-diffusive attached-growth units
       like bio-towers, thereby confirming both computational tractability and practical
       relevance.
By achieving these goals, the study aims to lay a foundation for next-generation modeling
tools that offer robust, interpretable, and scalable solutions for optimizing biological
wastewater treatment processes.

5   Significance of the Study

This study holds significant value on both theoretical and practical fronts by addressing
the critical limitations in existing modeling and optimization approaches for biological
wastewater treatment. First, it responds to the persistent issue of “data spillage,” whereby
models are inadvertently trained using the same performance indicators they are tasked
to predict. By proposing a rigorous strategy to separate input parameters from output
variables during model development, this research establishes a methodological
safeguard that greatly enhances the generalizability and reliability of predictions. The
avoidance of data spillage is particularly important in operational settings where overly
optimistic model performance often fails to translate into real-world success. Through its
emphasis on methodological integrity, the study’s contributions will help process
engineers make more dependable decisions about treatment system design and daily
operations.
Second, this work advances the field by reconciling advanced machine learning (ML)
techniques with mechanistic interpretability. While black-box ML algorithms such as
neural networks and ensemble methods can capture highly nonlinear relationships, they
often obscure the biochemical and microbial interactions that govern treatment
performance. By integrating fundamental biochemical and microbial principles into the
modeling architecture, this dissertation demonstrates how interpretability and domain
knowledge can be preserved without sacrificing predictive accuracy. This synergy is
crucial for engineers and researchers who not only need accurate predictions but also
require clear pathways to troubleshoot issues and refine plant operations based on
physical or biological insights. The enhanced interpretability opens the door to more
transparent design revisions, better-informed regulatory compliance, and more targeted
process improvements.
A third key contribution lies in the framework’s demonstrated scalability to large-scale or
superstructure configurations—a critical aspect for municipalities, industrial operators,
and research institutions seeking to optimize complex treatment networks. Existing
methods often falter when the dimensionality of a multi-unit system or superstructure
becomes too large, leading to insurmountable computational demands and convergence
problems. By employing linear or carefully linearized models that still respect the
underlying biochemical relationships, the proposed approach maintains computational
tractability, making large-scale optimization feasible. Notably, this framework also covers
advective-diffusive attached-growth systems like bio-towers, which have not yet been
modeled successfully by current ML techniques. Filling this gap extends the applicability
of next-generation modeling tools to a wider range of treatment processes, ultimately
helping operators achieve more holistic and efficient treatment designs.

6    Scope and Limitations of the Study

This dissertation is dedicated to the development and validation of a hybrid modeling and
optimization framework specifically designed for biological wastewater treatment
systems. The research focuses on integrating fundamental biochemical principles with
advanced machine learning (ML) techniques to achieve a balance between high
predictive accuracy and mechanistic interpretability. In particular, the framework is
demonstrated using two key treatment processes: (1) the activated sludge process,
representing suspended-growth systems, and (2) bio-towers, representing attached-
growth systems. These two processes collectively capture a broad spectrum of biological
treatment scenarios—ranging from conventional aeration basins to biofilm-based
reactors—and therefore serve as representative testbeds for evaluating the robustness,
scalability, and interpretability of the proposed modeling approach. Although limiting case
studies to these two processes excludes other potential treatment technologies (e.g.,
membrane bioreactors, sequencing batch reactors), they were selected because of their
widespread application, data availability in published literature, and capacity to illustrate
both suspended- and attached-growth phenomena.
A defining characteristic of this study is the reliance on experimental data sourced
exclusively from existing literature, rather than generating new experimental data. This
approach makes it possible to incorporate a diverse range of documented operational
scenarios and performance metrics from multiple studies, thereby enhancing the
framework’s potential applicability across various plant configurations and treatment
objectives. However, this choice introduces certain limitations. First, the quality and
consistency of the secondary data can vary considerably, influencing both the calibration
and validation phases of the modeling process. Ensuring compatibility and coherency
among datasets derived from disparate sources is an inherent challenge that must be
carefully managed to maintain methodological rigor. Second, the decision to focus on the
activated sludge process and bio-towers means that some advanced or specialized
wastewater technologies (e.g., membrane reactors or emerging hybrid systems) will not
be covered in depth. While this targeted approach is sufficient for illustrating the feasibility
and benefits of the proposed framework, it may limit generalizability to other treatment
processes that involve substantially different biochemical or operational complexities.

represent every conceivable variation found in real-world wastewater treatment practice.
