1    Introduction

Water pollution presents a critical environmental and public health challenge worldwide,
with particularly severe repercussions in developing nations (Lin et al., 2022; Madhav et
al., 2020). In the Philippines, over 90% of wastewater is discharged untreated into natural
water bodies annually (Tabios III, 2020). This leads to the degradation of aquatic
ecosystems, which pose risks to human health (NEDA & DOH, 2010; Tabios III, 2020).
Addressing this issue requires innovative and contextually appropriate wastewater
treatment technologies. Biological wastewater treatment is the most widely used
consortium of technologies worldwide to combat this issue (Duan et al., 2022; Hellal &
Abou-Elela, 2021; Liang et al., 2021; Rapi et al., 2021; Winkler & Straka, 2019) and is the
dominant approach applied for wastewater treatment in developing countries (Damalerio,
Beltran, Orbecido, et al., 2022; Machdar et al., 1997; Ulrich et al., 2009). There are two
objectives in biologically treating domestic wastewater. The first objective is to
substantially reduce its organic matter content, which inhibits the growth of heterotrophic
organisms (Marais & Ekama, 1976). This process prevents the potential spread of
disease and mitigates the depletion of O 2 in water bodies, which is a common
consequence of excessive heterotrophic activity. The secondary goal is to curtail the
concentration of eutrophic substances, such as NH4+, NO2⁻, and NO3⁻ (Marais & Ekama,
1976). This curtailment restricts the growth of autotrophic organisms and safeguards the
receiving water bodies from harmful algal blooms.

Designing biological wastewater treatment systems involves a hierarchy of
methodologies (Aboagye et al., 2021; Gu et al., 2023; Narayanan & Narayan, 2019;
Padrón-Páez et al., 2020; Rivas et al., 2008; Shojaei & Shojaei, 2021), each with unique
strengths and limitations. A general structure of the alternative computational design
methodologies employed in the literature is summarized in Figure 1-1. At the most
fundamental level, direct observations—such as monitoring microbial populations,
nutrient concentrations, sludge quality, and oxygen demand—provide the baseline data
necessary for any subsequent design work (Croll et al., 2023; Manav-Demir et al., 2024).
These observations feed into biophysical models that simulate key biochemical
processes (Henze et al., 2006). Widely recognized models, such as the activated sludge
models (ASM) (Henze et al., 2006), make it possible to predict effluent quality (Calise et
al., 2020; Szeląg et al., 2022), manage energy usage (Szeląg et al., 2022), and achieve
process stability (Caro et al., 2024; Jafarinejad, 2020) in biological wastewater treatment
systems. Initial design efforts often follow a trial-and-error approach, making minor
adjustments like altering retention times, aeration rates, or pH levels, to achieve
acceptable outcomes for the system (Sadri Moghaddam & Pirali, 2021). This approach is
often assisted with simulation software like GPS-X, Biowin, AQUASIM, SUMO, and
SIMBA (Bazemo et al., 2021; Bressani-Ribeiro et al., 2021; Caro et al., 2024; Jafarinejad,
2020; Sadri Moghaddam & Pirali, 2021).
However, as system complexity grows and optimization demands increase, exact
algorithms can be employed to mathematically search for globally optimal configurations
(Aboagye et al., 2021; Damalerio, Beltran, & Aviso, 2022; Padrón-Páez et al., 2020). In
practice, computational challenges and non-linearities in biological reactions limit the
feasibility of exact optimization algorithms, leading to the adoption of heuristic
optimization algorithms—like genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, or generalized
reduce gradient—that generate workable, locally optimal solutions (Gu et al., 2023; Jana
et al., 2022; B. Li et al., 2022; Niu et al., 2022; Rivas et al., 2008; Shojaei & Shojaei,
2021). To bridge the gap between computational complexity and accuracy, linear
approximations of non-linear models can be combined with exact algorithms, delivering
near-globally optimal designs that approach ideal performance without excessive
calculation times (Kim et al., 2009; Kusiak & Wei, 2012; M. Li et al., 2020; T. Yang et al.,
2014). Ultimately, depending on design requirements and resource constraints, the
selection of design methodology can yield outcomes ranging from basic “good-enough”
solutions to near-globally optimal ones, creating a spectrum of approaches that
wastewater engineers can strategically apply for the optimization of new or existing
treatment infrastructure.

A comparison of the alternative computational design approaches described above are
presented in Figure 1-2. Trial-and-error methods excel at providing quick fixes and
allowing operators to make incremental improvements based on empirical observations.
It is the most commonly applied approach in practice with the use of various simulation
software (Bazemo et al., 2021; Caro et al., 2024; Díez-Montero et al., 2019; Hellal & Abou-
Elela, 2021; Jafarinejad, 2020; Jasim, 2020; Kasi et al., 2011; Lainé et al., 1999; Liang et
al., 2021; Nazif et al., 2023; Sadri Moghaddam & Pirali, 2021; Simsek et al., 2012). Such
methods are manageable, but they often require substantial human effort and are labor-
intensive. On the opposite end, exact algorithms seek globally optimal configurations and
ensure that no superior solution remains undiscovered in an optimization routine that
seeks either optimal process design or control. Though computationally demanding,
these algorithms provide high precision and are best suited for well-defined and smaller-
scale problems, where linearity assumptions can be considered acceptable (Aboagye et
al., 2021; Damalerio, Beltran, & Aviso, 2022; Padrón-Páez et al., 2020). However, this
rarely happens when dealing directly with complex biochemical models of wastewater
treatment processes, which are often nonlinear due to Monod functions that define
reaction rates (Henze et al., 2006). Positioned between these extremes, heuristic
algorithms offer a pragmatic compromise by applying educated “rules of thumb” to
P a g e | 11

navigate large, complex, and highly non-linear scenarios (Poh et al., 2016). While they
may not guarantee a global optimum, they efficiently generate good solutions within
reasonable timeframes, making them popular for intricate systems where exact
approaches may be infeasible (Poh et al., 2016).
On the other hand, linear approximations present a way to simplify complex, non-linear
biological processes into more manageable models (Kim et al., 2009; Kusiak & Wei, 2012;
M. Li et al., 2020; T. Yang et al., 2014). These linear model approximations may directly
be calibrated using data that would otherwise be used to calibrate the nonlinear
biophysical models that are usually used in simulation software like Biowin, GPS-X,
AQUASIM, SUMO, and SIMBA (Bressani-Ribeiro et al., 2021; Hellal & Abou-Elela, 2021;
Kasi et al., 2011; Lainé et al., 1999; Liang et al., 2021). Unlike nonlinear biophysical
models, these methods can be integrated with exact optimization algorithms to achieve
near-globally optimal solutions without imposing excessive computational demands. This
balanced approach effectively combines accuracy with practicality. However, a notable
challenge is that linearization techniques commonly employed in the literature, such as
Taylor series expansions (M. Li et al., 2020; T. Yang et al., 2014), significantly restrict the
applicability of the models to the vicinity of the approximation point. Furthermore, while
such approaches are frequently utilized in process controller design (M. Li et al., 2020; T.
Yang et al., 2014), their application to optimal process design—such as wastewater
network optimization and process set-point determination—remains relatively rare.
Another linearization technique is through multivariable polynomial fitting (Ahn et al.,
2014; Kim et al., 2009; Lim et al., 2011). However, the current application of this approach
usually disregards the interdependence of compounds in biological wastewater treatment
systems, which is a major flaw. Kusiak & Wei (2012) was the first to address this issue
with a system of coupled nonlinear equations for the activated sludge process, but their
training dataset included target variables which constitute a major data spillage. Their
model is also nonlinear. At present, a competent linear model addressing the issues
presented above is not yet developed.
On the other hand, common machine learning (ML) methods, such as XGBoost,
LightGBM, random forests, support vector machines (SVM), and artificial neural networks
(ANNs), while widely used in soft sensing and optimal control (Croll et al., 2023; Fang et
al., 2011; Manav-Demir et al., 2024), are rarely utilized for approximating biophysical
models of biological wastewater treatment systems like, for example, bio-towers (Tyagi
et al., 2021). These ML methods yield nonlinear approximations, but they can still be
solved with greater ease than the original biophysical models using heuristic optimization
algorithms (Fang et al., 2011). For example, Fang et al. (2011) trained an SVM using
simulation data then performed optimization using the trained SVM model. A similar data-
driven application was performed by Asadi et al. (2017) but with random forests, artificial
neural networks, multi-adaptive regression spline, and k-nearest neighbors algorithms.
ML methods are suitable candidates for the approximation because they can
P a g e | 12

simultaneously perform predictions on multiple wastewater compounds. Multiple
response optimization has been emphasized as an important research direction in the
field of biological wastewater treatment (Nazlabadi et al., 2021).
On the other hand, ML method that generate linear models that can perform simultaneous
predictions on multiple wastewater compounds is presently unexplored in the literature
(Nazlabadi et al., 2021). An obvious candidate for such a method would be some new
implementation of the canonical regression technique, but no attempt is currently made
for applications such as approximating nonlinear biophysical models of biological
wastewater treatment systems. This research direction is interesting because it can
greatly improve on existing linear approximations like Taylor series expansion (M. Li et
al., 2020; T. Yang et al., 2014) and multivariable polynomial fitting (Ahn et al., 2014; Kim
et al., 2009; Lim et al., 2011); and would facilitate near-global optimization for either
process design or control unlike the previously mentioned ML models which are only
solvable using heuristic optimization algorithms that yield locally optimal solutions.
The special case of bio-towers as a potential application for these ML methodologies is
also interesting because the transport and biochemical reaction mechanisms of bio-
towers are governed by advection-diffusion-reaction (ADR) equations. A protocol for
approximating either the steady-state or unsteady state ADR equations of bio-towers by
ML methods is also an unexplored realm in the literature. ADR equations are notoriously
difficult to calculate within an optimization routine, so a protocol for its linear
approximation can greatly advance the efficiency of computational routines in simulation
and design software like Biowin, GPS-X, AQUASIM, SUMO, and SIMBA. In fact, none of
this software currently have built-in process design optimization features, which could be
attributed to the computational difficulty of calculating with the nonlinear biophysical
models of biological wastewater systems for which they are based on. Given these gaps,
the objectives of this study are:
      Investigate and compare existing ML algorithms for approximating nonlinear
       biophysical models of biological wastewater treatment systems.
      Develop a new method for generating a linear model that surpasses the ML
       algorithms mentioned above in terms of (i) computing time, (ii) prediction accuracy,
       and (iii) robustness when modeling both the activated sludge system (perfectly
       mixed, suspended growth system) and bio-tower system (advective-diffusive,
       attached growth system).
      Demonstrate the use of ML algorithms for optimal process design.

2    Theoretical Background

Central to biological wastewater treatment is the fact that continuous supply of matter and
energy is essential to support life through the creation and upkeep of cells. The key
elements involved in this life system include hydrogen (H), oxygen (O), carbon (C) and
nitrogen (N). Based on this, microorganisms involved in biological wastewater treatment
can be categorized based on the three main energy sources they derive from.
Photosynthetic autotrophs rely on radiation as their primary energy source. Heterotrophs
obtain energy from organic compounds. Chemical autotrophs use inorganic compounds
as their energy source.

A simplistic representation of the (bio)processes that microorganisms carry out in
biological WWTPs is illustrated in Figure 2-1. Solar radiation is the primary energy source
for the biosphere, and photosynthetic autotrophs capture this energy through
photosynthesis, converting CO₂ and water into glucose and releasing O₂, which supports
other life forms. These autotrophs also require nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus for
growth and metabolism, with nitrogen being essential for proteins and nucleic acids, and
phosphorous for ATP production. However, nutrient availability, especially in aquatic
ecosystems, can limit their growth. Heterotrophs, such as protozoa, fungi, and bacteria,
rely on consuming other organisms or organic matter for energy, which is used to
synthesize their cells, though some energy is lost as heat in the process. This results in
a decrease in available organic energy as it transfers between organisms, eventually
limiting heterotrophic life. Chemical autotrophs, which derive energy from the oxidation of
P a g e | 14

ammonia (NH₃/NH4+), nitrites (NO₂⁻), and nitrates (NO₃⁻), are crucial in wastewater
treatment due to their light-independent energy generation and ability to thrive in
environments with high carbon-to-nitrogen ratios and low light, unlike photosynthetic
autotrophs. In wastewater treatment, autotrophs typically refer to these chemical
autotrophs.
2.1   Organics Degradation
Heterotrophic energy metabolism entails the degradation of complex organic compounds
(CxHyOz), releasing CO2, protons (H+), and energy. CO2 is liberated as a gaseous
byproduct, while H+ is sequestered through association with a suitable acceptor in
reduction-oxidation (redox) reactions. Aerobic wastewater treatment employs
microorganisms that utilize O2 as the terminal electron acceptor during the oxidation of
organic compounds, a process known as aerobic respiration. The reaction equation
representing this process are as follows (Xie, 2021):

2.3   Denitrification

gaps, which are highlighted in the next section.

3     Review Related Literature

3.1    Foundational Activated Sludge Models
The Activated Sludge Model (ASM) family represents one of the most significant
developments in modern wastewater treatment engineering, providing a standardized
framework for simulating and designing biological treatment processes in activated
sludge systems (Henze et al., 2006). Initially developed under the aegis of the
International Association on Water Quality (IAWQ), which eventually became the
International Water Association (IWA), these models consolidated diverse research
efforts and practical experiences into a unified, mathematical representation of critical
microbial and chemical reactions (Henze et al., 2006). The need for such standardized
models arose due to the inherent complexity of biological wastewater treatment: multiple
microbial populations compete and cooperate to remove carbon, nitrogen, and,
eventually, phosphorus from the influent stream (Dold et al., 1981). By creating a common
platform for predicting effluent quality and guiding design modifications, the ASM family
fundamentally transformed both scientific inquiry and operational decision-making in
wastewater engineering (citation needed).(Dold et al., 1981; Henze et al., 1999; Nelson
& Sidhu, 2009; Vanrolleghem et al., 1999)
ASM1 was the first model in this series, introduced in 1987 by Henze, Grady, Gujer,
Marais, and Matsuo (Henze et al., 1987; Van Loosdrecht et al., 2015). It focused primarily
on carbon oxidation and nitrogen removal processes, capturing the essential dynamics
of heterotrophic and autotrophic biomass (Van Loosdrecht et al., 2015). Within ASM1,
heterotrophs metabolize readily biodegradable substrate and oxygen under aerobic
conditions, while they denitrify under anoxic conditions by using nitrate as an electron
acceptor (Henze et al., 2006). Autotrophs, on the other hand, nitrify ammonia to nitrite
and then to nitrate, representing one of the most critical steps in maintaining nitrogen
balance in effluents (Henze et al., 1987, 2006). The ability of ASM1 to predict effluent
quality for key parameters—such as chemical oxygen demand (COD), biochemical
oxygen demand (BOD), ammonia, and nitrate—rendered it a powerful tool for both
researchers and plant operators, even though the model did not explicitly address
phosphorus removal (Henze et al., 1999). Despite its limitations, ASM1’s relatively
straightforward approach to substrate utilization, biomass growth, and decay established
the core framework still evident in later models (Henze et al., 2006; Van Loosdrecht et
al., 2015).
Building on this foundation, ASM2 emerged in the mid-1990s to address enhanced
biological phosphorus removal (EBPR) processes by introducing phosphorus-
accumulating organisms (PAOs) (Henze et al., 1999). This expanded scope reflected the
growing interest among engineers and regulators in controlling eutrophication, a process
driven by excessive nutrient discharge into natural water bodies (Selerio, 2022). Under
P a g e | 22

ASM2, PAOs absorb phosphate during aerobic conditions and release it in anaerobic
phases, a dynamic that significantly improves phosphorus removal efficiency (Henze et
al., 1999, 2006). Though ASM2 retained the core processes of heterotrophic and
autotrophic metabolism from ASM1, it provided additional state variables and kinetic
expressions to capture the interplay between carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus
transformations. In subsequent refinements, ASM2d added the capacity for PAOs to
denitrify (i.e., use nitrate as an electron acceptor) under anoxic conditions, further
integrating nitrogen and phosphorus removal pathways (Henze et al., 1999). While the
inclusion of these processes offered a more comprehensive outlook, the expanded
parameter set demanded more extensive calibration data, which sometimes posed
challenges for real-world applications (Petersen et al., 2003; Vanrolleghem et al., 1999).
Meanwhile, ASM3, introduced around 2000, sought to streamline certain aspects of
ASM1 and ASM2 by revising the way hydrolysis and biomass growth were modeled
(Gujer et al., 1999). This model focused primarily on carbon and nitrogen removal,
returning to a simpler structure for plants not dealing with intense phosphorus standards
or where EBPR was less of a priority (Gujer et al., 1999). Specifically, ASM3 decoupled
the hydrolysis process from direct heterotrophic growth, allowing a more detailed
representation of how particulate organic matter is converted into soluble substrate (Gujer
et al., 1999; Henze et al., 2006). These refinements facilitated more accurate simulations
of sludge production and effluent quality under varying operational conditions, such as
fluctuating influent loads, changing temperatures, and intermittent aeration (Henze et al.,
2006). Still, ASM3 did not natively incorporate EBPR, meaning that engineers prioritizing
phosphorus removal often reverted to ASM2 or ASM2d (Hauduc et al., 2013; Henze et
al., 2006).
Across all ASM variants, the primary contributions to wastewater treatment design and
optimization include the ability to predict effluent quality under different operating regimes,
test hypothetical configurations before full-scale implementation, and guide the selection
of key parameters such as sludge retention time, aeration intensity, and reactor geometry
(Wang et al., 2024). Calibrating these models, however, requires extensive data on
wastewater characteristics, microbial community composition, and environmental
conditions, leading to potential challenges in practice (Petersen et al., 2003).
Furthermore, non-linear terms—such as Monod kinetics and complex stoichiometric
relationships—can pose computational hurdles, especially when operators or engineers
attempt to integrate ASM simulations with exact or heuristic optimization techniques (M.
Li et al., 2020; T. Yang et al., 2014). Nevertheless, the ASM family remains a cornerstone
of activated sludge research and design.

choice of model is therefore influenced by the treatment objectives, available operational
and analytical data, the extent of EBPR, and the level of detail required for process
optimization or research. In practice, practitioners often begin with simpler models like
ASM1 or ASM3 for broad design guidance (Hauduc et al., 2013) and switch to more

3.3    Advection-Diffusion-Reaction (ADR) Models
While the ASM family of models where originally used for modeling the activated sludge
process, which is a suspended-growth system, it has also been widely adopted for
modeling attached-growth systems such as biofilm technologies (Hellal & Abou-Elela,
2021; Lainé et al., 1999; Liang et al., 2021; Simsek et al., 2012). Bio-towers, sometimes
referred to as trickling filters, are among the most widely used attached-growth systems
for biological wastewater treatment and are particularly effective for removing organic and
nitrogenous pollutants from domestic and industrial effluents (Mahmoud et al., 2011,
2018; Tawfik et al., 2006, 2009; Tyagi et al., 2021). In these systems, a microbial biofilm
develops on a packed medium (e.g., rocks, structured plastic media) while wastewater
percolates through, enabling microorganisms to metabolize dissolved pollutants under
aerobic or anoxic conditions (Mahmoud et al., 2010, 2011; Nasr et al., 2022). Bio-towers
are generally appreciated for their relatively low operational costs and ability to handle
fluctuations in wastewater loading, although their design and performance optimization
can be complex due to the interplay between hydrodynamics, mass transfer, and
biological reactions (Bressani-Ribeiro et al., 2021; Nasr et al., 2022; Tyagi et al., 2021).
Accurately modeling these phenomena requires a rigorous description of fluid movement,
solute dispersion, and microbial transformations, which has led many researchers to
adopt Advection-Diffusion-Reaction (ADR) equations as the fundamental modeling
framework (Sadino-Riquelme et al., 2023).

A passively aerated bio-tower and its operation is illustrated in Figure 3-1. In this sample
setup, the wastewater is first pumped into the bio-tower, which elevates the fluid to a
specific column height. As it enters the system, it passes through a sprayer, which
distributes the wastewater evenly across the column. The bulk wastewater flows
downward through the column and is exposed to the biological layers within the bio-tower.
The treatment cycle that occurs in the system includes nitrification, an aerobic reaction
where ammonia (NH₄⁺) is converted to nitrite (NO₂⁻) and subsequently to nitrate (NO₃⁻).
This step is facilitated by nitrifying bacteria that thrive in oxygen-rich environments. As the
nitrified effluent moves through the system, it encounters anoxic zones where anammox
(anaerobic ammonium oxidation) bacteria are present. The anammox process enables
the conversion of ammonium and nitrite into nitrogen gas (N₂) without the need for
oxygen. This step is particularly effective in reducing nitrogen compounds and is essential
for minimizing nitrogen pollution. Following the anammox process, any remaining nitrate
and nitrite undergo denitrification in an anaerobic environment. Here, heterotrophic
denitrifying bacteria utilize the remaining nitrogen oxides (NO₂⁻ and NO₃⁻) as electron
acceptors and converting them into nitrogen gas (N₂), which is then released harmlessly
into the atmosphere. Additionally, organic nitrogen compounds are broken down by
heterotrophic bacteria through the process known as ammonification, which convert them
to ammonia.

In a bio-tower, several transport mechanisms facilitate the movement of contaminants,
nutrients, and gases within the system (Luan et al., 2023), which are illustrated in Figure
3-2. Diffusion allows oxygen and contaminants in bulk wastewater to enter the biofilm
layer. They move from high concentration areas in the bulk or air interface to lower
concentrations within the biofilm, which supply microorganisms with essential resources
for metabolism. At the same time, advection brings bulk wastewater and suspended
microorganisms closer to the biofilm carrier that facilitates contact. Microorganisms in the
bulk water then attach to the biofilm layer. As the biofilm thickens over time, parts of it
may detach, where microorganisms or biofilm fragments break off and return to the bulk
water. This detachment helps maintain biofilm density.

Despite the accuracy that detailed ADR models can provide, practical engineering
applications frequently require iterative trial-and-error design or heuristic optimization
approaches due to the high computational demands of solving large systems of coupled
partial differential equations (Sadino-Riquelme et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). When the
objective is to optimize bio-tower performance—whether by adjusting hydraulic loading
rates, selecting media configurations, or establishing control strategies—repeated ADR
simulations can be prohibitively time-intensive (Croll et al., 2023). Hence, researchers
have explored surrogate modeling techniques, including artificial intelligence and
machine learning, to approximate the results of full ADR models while reducing
computational load (Ching et al., 2022; Croll et al., 2023; Ekinci et al., 2023; Sundui et al.,
2021; Waqas et al., 2022). These surrogate models can guide system designers toward
near-optimal configurations in less time, thus bridging the gap between theoretical
accuracy and real-world feasibility (Sundui et al., 2021).
3.4   Software Packages for Modeling and Simulation
A range of specialized software has been developed for modeling and simulating
biological wastewater treatment processes, enabling engineers and researchers to study
treatment performance under a variety of operating conditions. Among the most
commonly used packages are GPS-X, Biowin, AQUASIM, SUMO, and SIMBA (Bazemo
et al., 2021; Caro et al., 2024; Díez-Montero et al., 2019; Hellal & Abou-Elela, 2021;
Jafarinejad, 2020; Jasim, 2020; Kasi et al., 2011; Lainé et al., 1999; Liang et al., 2021;
Nazif et al., 2023; Sadri Moghaddam & Pirali, 2021; Simsek et al., 2012). Each of these
platforms allows users to construct detailed representations of wastewater treatment
processes—often including activated sludge systems, fixed-film reactors (like bio-towers),
primary and secondary clarifiers, and additional unit operations. By offering pre-
configured templates of common processes, comprehensive libraries of kinetic models,
and graphical user interfaces, these software packages significantly reduce the effort and
expertise needed to set up sophisticated simulations [citation needed]. They typically
include modules for dynamic simulations, parameter estimation, and calibration, enabling
users to capture diurnal load variations, investigate chemical dosing strategies, or test the
impact of changes in aeration rates and sludge retention times.
GPS-X, developed by Hydromantis, is known for its intuitive interface and modular
approach, allowing users to drag and drop unit processes to build a virtual treatment plant
(Sadri Moghaddam & Pirali, 2021). It offers a variety of standard and advanced biological
models—such as ASM1, ASM2d, ASM3, and extensions for nitrification, denitrification,
and phosphorus removal—along with functionalities for process control testing and
scenario analysis (Jasim, 2020). Biowin, created by EnviroSim, similarly provides
comprehensive modeling features and is lauded for its user-friendly calibration routines
that guide practitioners in matching model predictions to real plant data (Caro et al., 2024;
Díez-Montero et al., 2019; Kasi et al., 2011; Simsek et al., 2012). AQUASIM, meanwhile,
P a g e | 34

was initially developed at Eawag (Swiss Federal Institute of Aquatic Science and
Technology) with a particular focus on controlling and analyzing aquatic systems; its
open-ended structure is conducive to customizing differential equations, making it
especially popular among researchers exploring unique or novel reactor configurations
(Bressani-Ribeiro et al., 2021). SUMO, by Dynamita, and SIMBA, by IFAK, are also
prominent: they incorporate models for activated sludge, biofilm reactors, and advanced
biological nutrient removal, frequently coupling these with optional extensions for
anaerobic digestion and side-stream processes (Bazemo et al., 2021; Jafarinejad, 2020;
Lainé et al., 1999; Liang et al., 2021; Nazif et al., 2023).
Despite the extensive functionalities of these platforms, embedded optimization
capabilities—particularly near-global optimization or advanced control—remain notably
absent or underdeveloped in most packages. The primary focus of these software
environments is on simulation, calibration, and scenario analysis; typically, engineers will
tweak parameters iteratively (often referred to as a “trial-and-error” approach) to find
reasonable solutions that meet regulatory standards (Croll et al., 2023). This manual or
semi-manual procedure can be sufficient when the primary aim is to ensure compliance
or gain insights into possible operational strategies. However, for formal optimization—
such as minimizing energy costs, maximizing treatment efficiency, or balancing multiple
objectives—users would need to embed these simulation tools inside an external
optimization framework, which can be cumbersome and computationally demanding
(Caro et al., 2024).
A reason for this gap is the computational complexity associated with wastewater
treatment models. Biological and physico-chemical processes in reactors like activated
sludge systems or bio-towers are inherently non-linear, often described using multiple,
interlinked partial differential equations (e.g., for advection-diffusion-reaction modeling)
that must be solved simultaneously (Díez-Montero et al., 2019; Kasi et al., 2011; Liang et
al., 2021; Sadino-Riquelme et al., 2023; Wik, 2003). These equations can be numerically
stiff and time-consuming to simulate, especially if the models include multiple state
variables (e.g., COD, BOD, nitrogen, phosphorus, and different microbial populations)
and dynamic conditions (like hourly fluctuations in influent loads) (M. Li et al., 2020; Wik,
2003; T. Yang et al., 2014). The software would have to iteratively run many simulations—
sometimes in the thousands, millions, or even billions—to fully explore the decision space
and converge on near-optimal solutions. Given these challenges, developers of packages
like GPS-X, Biowin, AQUASIM, SUMO, and SIMBA have, to date, concentrated on
making the simulation aspect robust and user-friendly rather than incorporating complex
built-in optimization engines that would risk long run times and limited performance on
standard computers.
Nevertheless, research groups and advanced users often couple these simulation tools
with external optimization frameworks—for instance, by writing scripts in platforms such
P a g e | 35

as MATLAB, Python, or specialized optimization software—to systematically evaluate
thousands of simulation scenarios (Caro et al., 2024). While this workflow can yield
powerful results, it typically demands significant computational power (e.g., high-
performance clusters) and specialized expertise in both modeling and optimization. The
lack of a direct, integrated optimization module within mainstream wastewater simulation
software remains a known limitation, especially for practitioners seeking straightforward,
single-click optimization solutions. Looking ahead, there is growing interest in surrogate
modeling or machine learning (ML) methods that can approximate complex simulation
outputs and thereby drastically reduce the computational burden, making embedded or
near-real-time optimization more feasible (J. Li et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024; Waqas et
al., 2022). If such surrogate or reduced-order models become more common, they may
be integrated into future releases of these software packages, potentially closing the gap
between simulation and embedded optimization in the wastewater treatment domain.
3.5   Optimization Problems
In the context of biological wastewater treatment, optimization problems typically involve
multiple, and often conflicting, goals: minimizing costs, conserving resources, and
ensuring compliance with regulatory standards for effluent quality. These objectives must
be addressed within the confines of practical limitations such as finite source capacities,
operational or technological constraints of treatment units, and uncertainties in pollutant
loads. Furthermore, diverse system configurations—from integrated water and
wastewater networks with various mixing and routing schemes to complex multi-stage
treatment plants—add layers of complexity to the decision-making process. By leveraging
optimization models, engineers and planners can systematically evaluate feasible system
configurations, assign process flows, and fine-tune operational parameters to identify the
most effective solutions. Three general optimization problems arise in wastewater
treatment literature, which are discussed here.

3.6       ML Models in Optimizing Biological Wastewater Treatment
Over the past two decades, the application of advanced machine learning (ML)
techniques to optimize biological wastewater treatment processes has gained
considerable momentum (Croll et al., 2023; Nazlabadi et al., 2021). Traditional process
optimization approaches often relied on mechanistic modeling and linear or polynomial-
based regression methods (Nazlabadi et al., 2021). However, the increasingly complex
dynamics of biological treatment systems—characterized by multiple interdependent
P a g e | 42

compounds and nonlinear interactions—have prompted researchers to explore more
flexible and data-driven ML models (Asadi et al., 2017). Notably, support vector machines
(SVM), XGBoost, LightGBM, random forests, multi-level perceptrons (MLP), artificial
neural networks (ANN), multi-adaptive regression splines (MARS), and k-nearest
neighbors (k-NN) have emerged as compelling alternatives due to their ability to handle
large datasets and capture highly nonlinear relationships among different process
variables (Asadi et al., 2017; Fang et al., 2011; Kusiak & Wei, 2012; Lim et al., 2011; Wu
et al., 2016).
One principal advantage of using ML algorithms in biological wastewater treatment is their
capacity to model complex processes without requiring a comprehensive mechanistic
understanding of the underlying biochemical reactions. Several studies illustrate this
benefit. For example, Asadi et al. (2017) harnessed real-world process data to create a
predictive model of the aeration process, a critical cost and energy driver in most
wastewater treatment plants. Similarly, Kusiak and Wei (2012) employed ML-based
approaches to optimize operational parameters in activated sludge processes, ultimately
demonstrating a potential reduction in both energy consumption and chemical usage.
These successes stem from the fact that ML models, through their sophisticated learning
algorithms, can identify subtle, nonlinear, and potentially high-order interactions between
factors such as oxygen transfer rates, influent loading, microbial community behavior, and
environmental conditions. A crucial step in developing robust ML models for wastewater
treatment optimization involves collecting sufficient and reliable training data. While some
research groups rely on actual plant or laboratory-scale operational data, others have
leveraged simulation packages like Biowin, GPS-X, AQUASIM, SUMO, and SIMBA (Ahn
et al., 2014; Fang et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2016). These software platforms offer pre-
calibrated mechanistic models of biological treatment processes, enabling researchers to
generate large volumes of data under various hypothetical operating conditions
(Bressani-Ribeiro et al., 2021; Caro et al., 2024; Díez-Montero et al., 2019; Hellal & Abou-
Elela, 2021; Jasim, 2020; Kasi et al., 2011; Liang et al., 2021). This simulation-based
approach can save time and resources compared to purely experimental data collection,
though it relies on the assumption that the simulation models are accurately calibrated
and reflective of real-world scenarios (Croll et al., 2023). The comprehensive datasets
derived from these simulations provide a broad parameter space that ML models can
learn from, enabling them to make more reliable predictions when faced with new or
unseen conditions.
Once adequately trained, ML models are typically embedded within broader optimization
frameworks that seek to identify the best set of decision variables, such as aeration rates,
recycle ratios, nutrient dosing schedules, or sludge retention times (Ahn et al., 2014; Fang
et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2016). Due to the highly nonlinear nature of many ML algorithms,
conventional gradient-based optimization methods can be less effective or even
inapplicable, as the solution space may be riddled with local minima or saddle points (Jain
P a g e | 43

& Kar, 2017). Consequently, researchers often resort to heuristic or meta-heuristic
optimization strategies, including genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimization, and
other evolutionary methods (Ahn et al., 2014; Fang et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2016). These
approaches can navigate complex and discontinuous objective functions and constraints,
offering “good-enough” solutions in a reasonable timeframe. However, they do not
guarantee global optimality and may exhibit issues with convergence, especially as the
search space becomes higher-dimensional or when the underlying ML model is extremely
complex (Jain & Kar, 2017).
Despite its popularity, especially in earlier studies (Fang et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2009; Lim
et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2016), multivariable response surface methodology (MRSM) is
scrutinized for inadequately addressing the interdependencies between different
compounds or performance indicators. Many MRSM models (Fang et al., 2011; Kim et
al., 2009; Lim et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2016) assume that each compound or treatment
performance metric can be optimized independently, effectively ignoring the coupled
biochemical reactions and microbial community interactions inherent in biological
systems. Such oversimplification can lead to suboptimal or misleading conclusions, as
changing one parameter (e.g., carbon source addition) to optimize the removal of a
particular compound (e.g., COD) may adversely affect the removal of another compound
(e.g., nitrogen). A related methodological pitfall appears in studies that attempt to capture
the interdependencies among wastewater constituents by including compound
concentrations (the target variables) directly in the training dataset as inputs (Asadi et al.,
2017; Kusiak & Wei, 2012). This practice can lead to “data spillage,” where the predictive
model inadvertently learns from outcomes that should be unknown during the training
phase. This can artificially inflate model accuracy and reduce generalizability to real
operational settings. When used in optimization, these flawed models risk recommending
untrustworthy operating conditions that fail to meet performance requirements when
deployed in practice. On the other hand, while advanced ML models excel at capturing
nonlinearities, they generally lack the mechanistic interpretability of simpler linear or
phenomenological models. Their “black-box” nature makes it challenging to derive
physical or biochemical insights, limiting the scope for process engineers to leverage
domain knowledge or identify fundamental process improvements (Croll et al., 2023).
Because biological wastewater treatment is inherently governed by microbial ecology,
biochemical kinetics, and mass transfer phenomena, the inability to interpret model
coefficients or internal architecture often complicates efforts to streamline or re-engineer
the process based on fundamental scientific principles. It should also be noted the no
application of any of the mentioned ML methods have been proven effective for modeling
Advective-Diffusive attached-growth systems like bio-towers, which is a major gap in the
literature.
Another challenge arises when extending optimization efforts beyond a single treatment
unit to multiple interlinked units, or a superstructure involving multiple sources, mixers,
P a g e | 44

and sinks such as those in Equation 3-1. In such scenarios, the number of decision
variables and constraints multiplies quickly, making the overall optimization problem
computationally expensive and prone to convergence issues—especially if a heuristic
optimizer is used in conjunction with a complex ML model. Ensuring that these large-
scale optimization problems remain tractable without compromising solution accuracy is
a prominent concern for real-world implementations. In light of these challenges, there is
a growing call for developing models that offer the best of both worlds: (i) the ability to
capture interdependencies among wastewater constituents, (ii) the avoidance of data
spillage, (iii) mechanistic interpretability, and (iv) predictive power that rivals or surpasses
that of nonlinear ML methods. A linear model that is carefully designed to reflect the
interactions of different compounds would address many of the criticisms leveled against
purely data-driven ML approaches (Nazlabadi et al., 2021). By incorporating terms or
factors that account for relevant biochemical relationships, such a model could maintain
interpretability—offering straightforward parameter coefficients tied to physical
phenomena—while still accurately predicting system behavior. This is especially critical
in large-scale optimization tasks, such as superstructure design, water reuse networks
(Equation 3-1), and multi-stage wastewater treatment plant designs (Equation 3-2),
where linear (or linearized) models are often necessary for computational tractability
(Nazlabadi et al., 2021). Furthermore, a linear model that respects the avoidance of data
spillage would ensure more rigorous calibration and validation processes. By carefully
segregating inputs and outputs—such that the target variables do not inadvertently enter
as model inputs—researchers could preserve the model’s integrity and bolster confidence
in its extrapolative capabilities. This methodological rigor would also facilitate clearer
integration of domain knowledge about microbial kinetics or stoichiometric relationships,
potentially bridging the gap between data-driven and mechanistic modeling approaches.

4   Statement of the Problem

Over the past two decades, various machine learning (ML) techniques have
demonstrated promise in modeling and optimizing biological wastewater treatment
processes. These approaches can capture nonlinear relationships among operational
parameters, biochemical reactions, and treatment performance indicators. However,
three main challenges persist:
Data Spillage and Oversimplification
Many existing studies include performance indicators (e.g., effluent concentrations) as
direct inputs to ML models, leading to “data spillage.” This undermines model
generalizability and inflates predictive accuracy in ways that do not hold in real operational
settings. Additionally, linear or polynomial models often fail to capture compound
interdependencies, ignoring the coupled biochemical reactions and microbial community
interactions critical to process performance.
Lack of Mechanistic Interpretability
Advanced ML methods (e.g., random forests, neural networks) deliver high predictive
power but function as “black boxes,” making it difficult to extract mechanistic insights. This
lack of transparency limits the ability of process engineers to incorporate domain
knowledge, improve fundamental processes, or troubleshoot effectively when real-world
performance deviates from model predictions.
Scalability and Model Gaps
When extending optimization to large-scale treatment superstructures with multiple units,
sources, mixers, and sinks, the dimensionality of the decision space escalates, causing
heuristic optimizers and complex ML models to struggle with convergence and tractability.
Moreover, no ML method has been validated for modeling advective-diffusive attached-
growth systems (e.g., bio-towers), leaving an important class of wastewater processes
insufficiently studied.
These gaps underscore the need for a modeling framework that:
       Reflects the interconnected nature of multiple wastewater constituents without
        causing data spillage,
       Maintains interpretability by integrating fundamental biochemical relationships,
        and
       Remains computationally tractable for large-scale or superstructure optimization
        tasks.
The research questions that this dissertation will answer are the following:
P a g e | 46

      How can ML-based models be designed to capture the interdependencies among
       multiple wastewater constituents without incurring data spillage, thereby improving
       predictive accuracy and generalizability?
      What modeling strategies or frameworks can balance the “black-box” nature of
       advanced ML methods with the mechanistic interpretability needed for practical
       process design and troubleshooting?
      How can these improved modeling approaches be scaled up to large, multi-stage
       or superstructure configurations—including advective-diffusive attached-growth
       systems—while ensuring computational feasibility and robust convergence in
       optimization?
The primary objective of this dissertation is to develop and validate a modeling and
optimization framework that bridges the gap between purely data-driven ML methods and
mechanistic models for biological wastewater treatment. Specifically, the proposed
approach will:
      Eliminate data spillage by rigorously separating input variables from performance
       metrics in model training and validation, ensuring more trustworthy predictions.
      Enhance mechanistic interpretability by incorporating biochemical and microbial
       process fundamentals into the modeling architecture, providing process engineers
       with actionable insights.
      Demonstrate scalability through the application of the framework to multi-stage
       systems and superstructures, including advective-diffusive attached-growth units
       like bio-towers, thereby confirming both computational tractability and practical
       relevance.
By achieving these goals, the study aims to lay a foundation for next-generation modeling
tools that offer robust, interpretable, and scalable solutions for optimizing biological
wastewater treatment processes.

6    Scope and Limitations of the Study

This dissertation is dedicated to the development and validation of a hybrid modeling and
optimization framework specifically designed for biological wastewater treatment
systems. The research focuses on integrating fundamental biochemical principles with
advanced machine learning (ML) techniques to achieve a balance between high
predictive accuracy and mechanistic interpretability. In particular, the framework is
demonstrated using two key treatment processes: (1) the activated sludge process,
representing suspended-growth systems, and (2) bio-towers, representing attached-
growth systems. These two processes collectively capture a broad spectrum of biological
treatment scenarios—ranging from conventional aeration basins to biofilm-based
reactors—and therefore serve as representative testbeds for evaluating the robustness,
scalability, and interpretability of the proposed modeling approach. Although limiting case
studies to these two processes excludes other potential treatment technologies (e.g.,
membrane bioreactors, sequencing batch reactors), they were selected because of their
widespread application, data availability in published literature, and capacity to illustrate
both suspended- and attached-growth phenomena.
A defining characteristic of this study is the reliance on experimental data sourced
exclusively from existing literature, rather than generating new experimental data. This
approach makes it possible to incorporate a diverse range of documented operational
scenarios and performance metrics from multiple studies, thereby enhancing the
framework’s potential applicability across various plant configurations and treatment
objectives. However, this choice introduces certain limitations. First, the quality and
consistency of the secondary data can vary considerably, influencing both the calibration
and validation phases of the modeling process. Ensuring compatibility and coherency
among datasets derived from disparate sources is an inherent challenge that must be
carefully managed to maintain methodological rigor. Second, the decision to focus on the
activated sludge process and bio-towers means that some advanced or specialized
wastewater technologies (e.g., membrane reactors or emerging hybrid systems) will not
be covered in depth. While this targeted approach is sufficient for illustrating the feasibility
and benefits of the proposed framework, it may limit generalizability to other treatment
processes that involve substantially different biochemical or operational complexities.


Additionally, while the study leverages a wide range of operational conditions, it cannot
represent every conceivable variation found in real-world wastewater treatment practice.
This selective coverage is necessary to maintain a focused scope and to demonstrate
depth of analysis in the target domains; nevertheless, it also highlights the need for future
research to extend or adapt the framework to other treatment technologies. The
computational feasibility of the proposed optimization algorithms presents another
P a g e | 50

constraint. Although the framework is designed to handle large-scale systems—
especially superstructures that involve multiple sources, sinks, and interconnected
units—practical deployment for real-time optimization or extremely large configurations
may require advanced computing resources that exceed the scope of this work.

power. While the hybrid approach strives to merge mechanistic insights with data-driven
modeling to facilitate transparency, certain black-box ML algorithms might deliver slightly
higher predictive accuracy for highly specialized tasks at the expense of clarity about

understanding for effective troubleshooting and process optimization. Furthermore,
although the study includes bio-towers as a representative of advective-diffusive

treatment plants imply that further calibration and customization may be required to
realize optimal performance in specific operational contexts.
Despite these constraints, the scope of this dissertation is well-justified by the pressing
need to develop a robust, scalable, and interpretable modeling framework that addresses
critical gaps in current wastewater treatment research. By focusing on both suspended-
growth (activated sludge) and attached-growth (bio-towers) processes, and by employing

situations while also laying a solid foundation for future extensions.

7.1   Coupled Linear Equations Framework for Optimization (CLEFO)
The biochemical processes involved in biological wastewater treatment are inherently
complex, characterized by multiple interdependent constituents, including various
compounds and biomass concentrations. Here, we propose a coupled linear equations
framework for optimization tailored to encapsulate the intricate interdependencies and
interaction effects inherent in biological wastewater treatment systems. The primary
objective of this model is to provide a tractable yet comprehensive framework that
captures the dynamic relationships among active wastewater constituents, specifically
compound and biomass concentrations, while accounting for the influence of key
operational parameters such as flow rate, media porosity, and influent compound
concentrations without loss of tractability in a global optimization routine.

7.5      Multi-Stage, Multi-Unit Network Optimization Framework
The single-unit CLEFO optimization model for wastewater treatment is extended to
accommodate a multi-stage, multi-unit framework that captures the complexities of
sequential treatment processes, which is required to solve the problem detailed in
Equation 3-2. In this extended model, the wastewater undergoes 𝑆 sequential treatment
stages 𝑠 = 1, 2, … , 𝑆, with each stage 𝑠 capable of employing one among 𝑈𝑠 alternative
process units 𝑢 ∈ 𝒰𝑠 . This selection is governed by binary decision variables 𝑦𝑠,𝑢 ,
ensuring that exactly one unit is chosen per stage, thereby constructing a superstructure
of feasible unit configurations across all stages.
The model incorporates three distinct types of independent variables 𝑋 namely, the
influent compound concentrations (𝑋 𝐼 ), flow variables (𝑋 𝐹 ), and process-unit-specific
                                                                                   𝐼
variables (𝑋 𝑃 ). For the first stage (𝑠 = 1), influent compound concentrations 𝑋𝑠,𝑖  represent
the raw wastewater concentrations of compound 𝑖 ∈ ℐ. For subsequent stages (𝑠 > 1),
the influent concentrations are directly linked to the effluent concentrations from the
                             𝐼
preceding stage, i.e., 𝑋𝑠,𝑖     = 𝑌𝑠−1,𝑖 . On the other hand, the value of volumetric flow
variables 𝑋𝑓𝐹 are decided exclusively at the first stage for 𝑓 ∈ ℱ are held constant across
all stages to maintain consistent flow rates throughout the treatment process. Lastly,
                                     𝑃
process-unit-specific variables 𝑋𝑢,𝑗      and 𝑗 ∈ 𝒥 pertain to operational parameters unique to
each process unit, such as aeration rates or chemical dosages – depending on the
treatment mechanism of the unit. They are activated only if their corresponding unit 𝑢 is
selected in stage 𝑠.

7.6   McCormick Envelopes
It is apparent that the following linearization employed on bilinear terms of the multi-stage,
multi-unit framework makes the model large due to the multiple linearization steps of the
method and its introduction of binary variables and constraints at each step, which begins

i.e., (𝑤1 )2 − (𝑤2 )2 , and then the squared terms are linearized using piecewise linear
approximation through the auxiliary 𝑇 variables, i.e., (𝑤1 )2 ↦ 𝑇1 , (𝑢2 )2 ↦ 𝑇2. For large
applications, the linearization may render the model more computationally costly that its
original nonlinear model. We address this issue by employing a linear relaxation using
McCormick envelopes (McCormick, 1976). We know that 𝑥 and 𝑦 are continuous

8     Methodology

8.1       Data
Bressani-Ribeiro et al. (2021)’s experimentally validated AQUASIM model is used to
emulate a sponge-bed bio-tower. The model is used to simulate a thousand operational
scenarios for influent volumetric flow rate, cross-sectional area, column height, sponge
porosity, temperature, and influent compound concentrations. The bio-tower is divided
into ten compartments. The compartment influent and compartment effluent compound
concentrations are recorded for all compartments. At the end of the simulations, there are
ten thousand data points that is used to calibrate the CLEFO model.
On the other hand, Fall et al. (2015)’s experimentally validated AQUASIM model is used
to emulate a conventional activated sludge process. The model is used to simulate ten
thousand operational scenarios for influent volumetric flow rate, recirculated activated
sludge (RAS) flow, aeration tank volume, settler volume, and influent compound
concentrations. At the end of the simulations, there are ten thousand data points that is
used to calibrate the CLEFO model.
The Monte Carlo method is used to perturb the operational variables considered in both
wastewater treatment technologies using a uniform distribution. The distribution bounds
of the operational variables for each wastewater treatment technology are presented in
Table.
On top of performing simulation-based case studies on the bio-tower and the conventional
activated sludge model. We also perform a case study using actual data from 527 daily
measures of sensors in an urban wastewater treatment plant employing a conventional
activated sludge process from Poch (1993) of the University of California Irvine (UCI)
Machine Learning Repository.
The main datasets are divided into calibration (70%) and validation (30%) sub-datasets.
Mean-value imputation is executed for the dataset from Poch (1993). StandardScaler
from Python’s sklearn library is used to rescale the datasets. The rescaling is separately
executed for each sub-dataset.
Lastly, cost data and other economics-related data are obtained from the literature. At
present, the data is not yet curated, but such data should be manifold.
8.2       Comparison with Common Machine Learning (ML) Techniques
The CLEFO model is calibrated using ordinary least squares regression. The following
ML models are calibrated using the same calibration datasets used for the CLEFO model:
          K-nearest Neighbors (KNN),
          Light Gradient-Boosting Machine (LightGBM),
          Artificial Neural Network (ANN),
P a g e | 67

          Random Forest (RF),
          Support Vector Machine (SVM),
          Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost).
The calibrated models are then tested using the validation datasets, which they’ve never
seen before. They are tasked to predict the effluent compound concentrations given the
operational variables as the model inputs. The accuracy of their predictions is then
compared. This comparison establishes whether CLEFO model is at par or better than
common ML techniques at modeling wastewater treatment technologies.
Since CLEFO model is calibrated using ordinary least squares regression, we expect it
to be the most computationally competitive technique compared to the common ML
techniques, which are all notoriously computationally costly to implement, especially
when the calibration dataset is large. Furthermore, we expect the CLEFO model to be
more accurate at predicting effluent compound concentrations than the common ML
techniques because it is modeled in view of the nature of microbial stoichiometry, which
the black-box ML techniques do not consider.
8.3       Evaluating Optimization Models
The first evaluation is carried out between the linear and nonlinear single-unit CLEFO
models. The second evaluation is carried out between the exact linear, McCormick, and
nonlinear multi-stage multi-unit CLEFO models. The evaluations are carried out to
compare the objective function values and computational gains from the linearization
protocols at various model sizes. For the case of the single-unit CLEFO models, the
model size is determined exclusively by the number of dependent and independent
variables considered in the model. On the other hand, the model size of the multi-stage
multi-unit CLEFO models is exclusively determined by the number of treatment stages,
alternative wastewater treatment technologies per stage, dependent variable, and
independent variables considered in the model.
We expect that the linear single-unit CLEFO model will be superior to its nonlinear
counterpart on both objective function values and computational efficiency at all model
sizes because there is not many variables or constraints involved when executing
optimization on a single unit of wastewater treatment technology. Furthermore, although
the linear single-unit CLEFO model has more decision variables (both continuous and
binary) and constraints than its nonlinear counterpart, its linearity provides it with great
computational advantages due to the existence of extremely powerful linear solvers.
On the other hand, we expect that the nonlinear multi-stage multi-unit CLEFO model will
be superior to all its linear counterparts on computational efficiency when the model size
is small, i.e., when there are only two stages and two process units per stage. The linear
multi-stage multi-unit CLEFO model have a considerable number of binary variables,
especially the exact linear model, which I expect will consistently be worse performing
P a g e | 68

than either the nonlinear or the McCormick linear model because of the sheer number of
its binary variables and constraints. However, we expect the McCormick linear multi-stage
multi-unit CLEFO model to be superior on computational efficiency against the nonlinear
and exact linear models when the model size is big. In most practical wastewater
treatment design planning applications, there will be many wastewater treatment stages
and alternative wastewater treatment process units per stage, which makes a superior
model in such applications indispensable.
8.4   Application of Optimization Models
The single-unit CLEFO model can be easily applied to determine the optimal values for
the operational design variables of sponge-bed bio-towers and activated sludge
processes using the datasets described previously. First, the CLEFO model needs to be
calibrated using the datasets, then the calibrated model is used for the single-unit
optimization application.
The multi-stage, multi-unit CLEFO model requires that we have calibrated CLEFO models
for all wastewater treatment process units considered in the design problem. To this end,
we use the process units defined in the wastewater treatment case studies in the
AQUASIM package presented in Figure 8-1. We assume that there are two treatment
stages, i.e., primary treatment and secondary treatment stages. The first stage is either
an activated sludge process which we define using datasets from Poch (1993) or an up-
flow anaerobic sludge blanket which we define using datasets from sludgeb.aqu. The
second stage is either a membrane bioreactor which we define using datasets from
xylene.aqu or sponge-bed biotower which we define using datasets from Bressani-Ribeiro
et al. (2021).
